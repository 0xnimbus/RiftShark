# Training, 2026-11-10 (after gen 14)

Gen 14 (masked judge: no card identities of our hand or board) is the benchmark. All gates: 200 mirror games vs
`learned:gen_014`, seeds 90000, 55% needed to promote.

| Candidate | Data | Score |
|---|---|---|
| cand_g15lin (linear) | gens 6-8 + 1000 cand_mhb games + 3000 gen-14 self-play games | 45.5% |
| cand_g15nn (wide-and-deep, 16 hidden) | same | 45.5% |
| cand_g15on (linear) | only the 3000 gen-14 games | 39.5% |
| cand_g15bd (linear) | gens 6-8 + turn-boundary rows of the 3000 gen-14 games (no what-if rows) | 46.0% |
| cand_g15mix (linear) | gens 6-8 + 2400 mixed self-play games (gens 14/8/6/hand-written score, epsilon 0.06) | 51.0% |
| cand_g15mix2 (linear) | gens 6-8 + both mixed runs (second: gens 14/8/6/3/score, epsilon 0.12), 6571 games | 44.0% |

Findings:
- A bigger model does not help: the 16-unit net learned nothing over the linear judge (val loss 0.5232 vs 0.5235)
  and played the same 91/200.
- More self-play data from gen 14 makes the judge worse, and the more of it, the worse (gen-14 data alone: 39.5%).
  It is not the what-if rows (dropping them: 46%). Gen 14 was trained on the games of older, card-aware AIs
  (gens 3-8); its own games seem to cover too narrow a set of positions to learn from.
- Mixed self-play (each seat picks one of gens 14 / 8 / 6 / the hand-written score, twice the exploration:
  `train_loop selfplay --policy a,b,c --epsilon 0.06`) no longer hurts: 51%, a tie. Varied games are what the judge
  needs; gen 14's own games are too uniform.
- A second mixed run (gen 3 added, epsilon 0.12) trained with the first: 44%, although its validation loss is the
  best so far (0.496). So 51% was noise, and the pattern holds for every data change since gen 8: a judge that
  predicts results better does not rank options better. More value data for the linear judge is exhausted as a lever.
- Container restarts lost one self-play checkpoint written to /mnt/project-files; runs now checkpoint under `out/`.

## 2026-11-11: two lookahead fixes for reactive play (Riven, Kennen)

`tools/deck_vs.py DECK A B --games 20`: the deck played by A against the 10 other decks played by gen 14, 200 games,
same seeds for every row (results in `docs/results/deck_ai_2026-11-11/`).

| AI playing the deck | Riven | Kennen | Mirror gate vs gen 14 |
|---|---|---|---|
| gen 14 (baseline) | 58.0% (51-65) | 48.0% (41-55) | - |
| `learned:gen_014:rw`: in the opponent's turn, a reaction is judged after the opponent plays out the rest of its turn (we may answer again) instead of as if it then ends its turn | 55.3% (48-62) | 50.5% (44-57) | 47.5% |
| `learned:gen_014:hold`: on our turn with a fast spell in hand, the best 3 options and "end turn" are judged after the opponent's whole turn (one-sample heuristic AIs) | 35.2% (29-42) | stopped | - |

- `rw`: only about 2 reaction windows per game have a real option (the AI rarely keeps energy up), so the change is
  small either way. No gain.
- `hold`: much worse. Two turns played by the one-sample heuristic AI are a noisy, biased judge; the deeper look
  mostly favours ending the turn and Riven loses tempo. The lesson matches learned-mcts and learned-deep: a deeper look
  only helps with an opponent model about as strong as the AI itself, and that one is too slow to run inside a search.
- Both stay available as policy options (off by default).
