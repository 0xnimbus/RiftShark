# AI v2, steps 1-2: policy net and two-turn search (2026-11-11/12)

Plan: docs/plans/ai_v2.md. Benchmark: gen 14 (`learned:gen_014`). Gates are mirror matches against gen 14, seed 90000
(a copy of gen 14 scores exactly 50% there).

## Step 1: policy net (a fast copy of gen 14)

- Data: 1500 gen-14 self-play games recorded with every option of every decision (`tools/policy_data.py`), 160,801
  decisions with two or more options.
- Model: `riftsim/core/learn/policy_net.py`, one hidden tanh layer (64) over the position features and 168 option
  features (kind, hashed card identity, cost, stats, destination). Trained by `tools/train_policy.py`.
- Agreement with gen 14 on held-out games: 61% (chance 30%); 68% on main-phase action decisions.
- Playing alone (`policy:policy_g14`) vs gen 14: **18%** (100 games). About 100x faster per move.

## Step 2: two-turn search with the policy net as the opponent model

`learned:gen_014:p=policy_g14[:psN][:b=X][:dN][:crn]`: gen 14's one-turn judgement ranks the options; the top 3
(plus end turn / pass) are played on determinised copies through the opponent's whole turn (opponent model inside,
our answers too) and judged at the start of our next turn.

| Variant | Opponent model in the search | Score vs gen 14 | Games |
|---|---|---|---|
| deep value decides | policy net | 27% (16/60, stopped) | 60 |
| blend 0.5 shallow + deep, 3 samples | policy net | 44% | 100 |
| deep value decides | gen 14 judging only the policy net's top 3 options (`ps3`) | 35% | 100 |
| same, common random numbers (`crn`) | `ps3` | 41.7% | 100 |
| same, common random numbers, blend 0.5 | `ps3` | 47.4% | 100 |

## Why the deeper look loses: noise

A diagnostic (8 samples per candidate, same determinisations for every candidate) at gen-14 decisions: the value of
one candidate after the opponent's turn varies with the sample by **sd 70-115** (in 1000 x (win prob - 0.5) units), and
the difference between two candidates on the *same* samples still varies by sd 20-190. With 3 samples the error of a
difference is ~15-110, while the gaps between gen 14's top three options are usually 10-60. So the two-turn value
mostly picks at random among the top candidates, and throws away the one-turn ranking that is reliable (its own
noise is small because only the rest of our turn is simulated). Getting the error below the gaps needs ~30+ samples
per candidate, i.e. ~10x the cost of the current searching AI (already ~5x gen 14) in pure Python.

The opponent model was not the bottleneck: a stronger one (`ps3`, close to gen 14) did no better than the policy net.

## What this means for the plan

Sampling the opponent's whole turn at move time is too noisy at our compute. The deeper information has to come
from training instead: label positions with many-sample (or real-game) outcomes offline and teach the judge, so the
one-turn search stays cheap and low-noise. That is plan step 4 (a judge that sees card identities, trained on more
data); step 3 (expert iteration) needs a search that beats gen 14 first.
